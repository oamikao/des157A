Skills in Visual Analysis

October 25, 2026

I really enjoyed looking at the photos in the NYTimes Article, it talks about something that we constantly do in everyday life, visual analysis and tying details into a larger observation. Creating meaning from just a glance is an important skill, not only identifying visual references but also tying it into global/cultural values allows people to become stronger learners. I've taken some art history classes where the main focus is to hone these skills, and oftentimes its hard to look at images/objects in that lens. We are always so used to describing what we see objectively but now what we feel intuitively, which is the biggest part of visual analysis: tying in those feelings into an argument with evidence of what we see.

The article mentions that learning this skill is easier when the object of analysis is something that people are interested in, and images are definitely the best start to this: visually stimulating and full of narratives at just a glance, compared to texts that need more effort to understand. Ultimately, it is also of the designers best interest to work with images that produce this want to know more. The best photos invite curiousity and meaning, are subjective and can be viewed through many lens, using the environment and its subjects as a way to create conversations.

Best Practices for using Overlays

October 14, 2026

Overlays can be helpful when it comes to displaying small amounts of content for various needs. Whether to be unintrusive on a page, time sensitive, or for collecting certain user data, overlays are usually the way to go. As a user however, its easy to notice when overlays become unnecessary. I personally find that deceptive overlays, one that hide an exit button or redirect you to a different page when you mean to click out are frustrating. Ones that immediately pop up to advertise content on a site are equally frustrating, adding a barrier to the actual site content and creating extra steps. Like mentioned in the article, overlays that are not user initiated are tricky to navigate, as even important/urgent information may be disregarded if displayed on screen too obtrusively.

I usually don't like when websites have a lot of overlays, so when it comes to sites I would design, they would be kept to an absolute minimum, and hopefully only viewed when wanted to by a user. Important information that needs to be relayed should usually be prioritized in its own page or directly onto the site so that users don't need to take the extra step to access everything else.

Best Practices for Form Design

October 5, 2026

Forms are one of my biggest pet peeves when it comes to web design, as a lot of them fail to be simple. The article on form design by Salim Ansari highlights many of the common issues, especially ones that ask complicated questions first, overwhelming the user with information before they decide to commit to it. I find this issue emphasized in Google forms, where you cannot go to the next page of a form without filling in all the details of the current one. It's frustrating when you want a quick overview of what the form needs, and definitely turns users away immediately. A lot of successful form design means being accessible and clear. Laying out information that is easily digestible in a quick glance and making input directions clear helps create seamless forms, as I find another big struggle is knowing what the form wants especially when it doesn't provide examples or extra information.

Forms are usually one of the first intentional points of interaction between a user and a product/brand, whether it be subscriber lists, shipping forms or applications, so clear feedback, input examples and simple organization create a great first impression. If the goal is to bring users back to a site, accessibility and readability are important first steps to recognize what makes a user stay in the first place.

An example of a good form practices is that of WWF's ecard form. It includes placeholder text in the boxes that describe what is needed but also label each field, emphasizing to the user what information to put even once they start typing. Each option to select is described so that the user is informed about what decision they are making, and every bit of info needed is required yet simple to avoid overwhelming the user. The feedback once submitted is also clear, explaining what information needs to be updated and how.
